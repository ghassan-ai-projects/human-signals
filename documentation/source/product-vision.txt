Yes. The right way to use **working backwards** here is not to ask, “What features should we build?” but:

> “If this became the most admired learning product in human physiology, what would people say was uniquely great about it?”

Then we treat those future statements as product requirements.

I pressure-tested the concept through four lenses: **learning science, product differentiation, interaction design, and scientific credibility**. The strongest version is more ambitious than a 3D hormone encyclopedia.

Here is the imagined future.

# Human Signals Wins Global Learning Honors by Turning Human Physiology Into Something You Can See

**The interactive learning platform has done for hormones and neurotransmitters what Google Earth did for geography.**

When Human Signals launched, its premise sounded simple: put a three-dimensional human body in the center of the screen and let people explore hormones, neurotransmitters, organs, brain regions and physiological systems.

But that description misses what made the product remarkable.

Human Signals does not teach the human body as a collection of facts. It teaches it as a living network of causes and consequences.

Select cortisol and the experience begins in the brain. The hypothalamus illuminates, CRH travels toward the pituitary, ACTH continues toward the adrenal glands, cortisol enters the circulation, and the organs it affects appear across the body.

Select dopamine and the camera moves seamlessly into the brain, revealing separate pathways involved in movement, motivation, endocrine regulation and learning.

Select “stress,” “sleep,” “fasting,” “exercise” or “sexual response,” and multiple systems begin changing together over time.

The result feels less like reading a textbook and more like being allowed inside the human body.

## Learning by following the signal

The breakthrough is Human Signals' deceptively simple learning model:

**Trigger → Source → Signal → Target → Effect → Feedback**

Every topic follows that structure.

Rather than telling students that ACTH is produced by the pituitary, the system shows why ACTH appeared in the first place, where it travels, what it causes, and how the resulting cortisol eventually feeds back into the system.

This emphasis on causal relationships has become one of Human Signals' most copied ideas.

The product's designers call it the **Signal Journey**.

Students simply call it “the thing that finally made hormones make sense.”

## A body you explore, not a diagram you memorize

At the center of the product is an interactive 3D human model.

The learner can rotate the body, move through layers and zoom continuously from the whole organism into individual organs, brain structures and signaling pathways.

The body is deliberately simplified. Human Signals resisted the temptation to reproduce every anatomical structure.

Instead, complexity appears only when it becomes useful.

A beginner might initially see the brain, pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas and reproductive organs.

Zoom into the brain and additional structures appear.

Enter an advanced pathway and receptors, feedback loops and molecular mechanisms become visible.

This concept of **progressive scientific depth** has made the same product useful to curious adults, secondary-school students, medical students and even clinicians refreshing concepts outside their specialties.

## The most popular feature begins with a strange question

One of the most celebrated parts of Human Signals is called **Human States**.

Instead of asking learners to begin with the name of a molecule, the product lets them begin with an experience:

“What happens when I become stressed?”

“What happens when I fall asleep?”

“What happens after I eat?”

“What happens during prolonged fasting?”

“What happens during sexual arousal and orgasm?”

The body then becomes a dynamic timeline.

Signals rise and fall, organs activate, feedback loops appear and different timescales become visible.

Crucially, Human Signals avoids pretending that biology is deterministic. When evidence is uncertain, variable or context-dependent, the interface says so.

That scientific restraint has become as important to the product's reputation as its visual design.

## The tiny button students keep pressing

Beside almost every important relationship is a small button:

**Why?**

Why does cortisol increase glucose availability?

Why does dopamine suppress prolactin?

Why does melatonin rise at night?

Why does insulin reduce blood glucose?

Pressing the button reveals the mechanism.

Another “Why?” often appears inside the answer.

Users can therefore move backward through a causal chain until they reach the level of explanation they need.

Teachers have described the interaction as “curiosity made navigable.”

## It refuses to call dopamine the happiness chemical

Human Signals has also received praise for what it refuses to simplify.

Dopamine is not presented as a “pleasure hormone.”

Oxytocin is not simply the “love hormone.”

Serotonin is not reduced to “the happiness chemical.”

Instead, the application shows different pathways, receptors, contexts and functions.

Every important relationship carries an evidence indicator ranging from strongly established physiology to emerging or context-dependent evidence.

Primary references are never far away.

The result is unusually accessible without becoming misleading.

## From memorization to mental models

Perhaps Human Signals' greatest achievement is that users begin predicting what will happen before the animation shows them.

After learning several signaling systems, the learner begins recognizing recurring patterns:

a signal stimulates another signal;

a target organ produces an output;

that output feeds back upstream;

multiple systems interact;

timing matters;

and biological effects depend on receptors and context.

At that moment, learners are no longer memorizing hormones.

They are learning how the body thinks.

That transformation is why Human Signals has swept international awards for learning design, scientific visualization and educational technology.

It did not make physiology smaller.

It made physiology understandable.

# One major addition from the backward exercise: prediction

This was not in our original design, and now I think it should eventually become one of the most important features.

After learning a few pathways, sometimes stop the animation:

```text id="5lfzks"
Stress
   ↓
Hypothalamus
   ↓
CRH
   ↓
Pituitary
   ↓
        ?

What happens next?

[A] Insulin
[B] ACTH
[C] Melatonin
[D] Dopamine
```

Or more sophisticated:

> Cortisol has become elevated. What should happen to CRH if the feedback system is functioning normally?

Now learning becomes active.

Then the animation continues.

That's significantly better than quizzes detached from the learning interface.

**Key references**

**What remains uncertain**

This could differentiate Human Signals enormously from wellness websites and social-media hormone content.

# Quality bar

Before calling a screen finished, I would judge it against these ten criteria:

| Criterion                 | Question                                                                           |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Causality**             | Does the learner understand *why* one event leads to another?                      |
| **Spatial understanding** | Does 3D location genuinely improve understanding?                                  |
| **Time**                  | Can the learner understand what happens first, later and for how long?             |
| **Interaction**           | Is exploration more effective than reading the equivalent paragraph?               |
| **Progressive depth**     | Can a beginner understand it without blocking an advanced learner?                 |
| **Scientific honesty**    | Are uncertainty, context and evidence represented correctly?                       |
| **Mental model**          | Will the learner recognize the same pattern elsewhere?                             |
| **Recall**                | Is the concept likely to remain understandable tomorrow without rote memorization? |
| **Curiosity**             | Does one concept naturally invite exploration of another?                          |
| **Restraint**             | Have we removed everything visually impressive that doesn't improve learning?      |

I would set a very high rule:

> **If an animation is merely beautiful, remove it. If the animation explains something difficult that text struggles to explain, keep it.**

And the bar for a flagship lesson should probably be **9/10 across this table before we consider it done**.
